THE SHORT VERSION

Aircraft constantly broadcast who and where they are. This round screen listens for those broadcasts, works out where each airplane sits relative to this office, and paints it on a radar scope. Nothing here is simulated or replayed — if a plane is on the glass, it is overhead.

THE SIX THINGS ON SCREEN

HOW TO USE ITPAGE 2 OF 3
TAP AN AIRCRAFT

Who is that?

Opens the flight-detail card: callsign, airline name and logo, where it came from and where it is going, aircraft type, altitude and speed. Swipe down or right to go back.

PRESS & HOLD ~3½ SECONDS

Only if the Wi-Fi changes

Puts the device into Wi-Fi setup so it can be pointed at a new network. Harmless by accident — it gives up after five minutes and reconnects on its own.

LAST RESORT

Blank, frozen, or plainly sulking?

Unplug the USB-C cable, count to five, plug it back in. It finds the Wi-Fi and rebuilds the picture within a minute or so, and no settings are lost by restarting it.

HOW IT WORKSPAGE 3 OF 3

Why does it sweep like that?

A real radar has a physical dish that can only look one way at a time, so its display refreshes a target only when the beam comes round to it. This device already knows where everything is — it withholds the update deliberately, and repaints each aircraft as the arm crosses its bearing. That is the whole illusion, and it is why a blip can sit motionless for a moment and then jump.

The honest limitation: this is not really a radar. It cannot see anything that isn't voluntarily broadcasting — notably some military aircraft, and light aircraft old enough to carry no transponder.

It carries its own aircraft-type database, airport list and town map in flash, so the silhouettes and place names need no internet at all. Only live traffic, weather and route lookups go out to the network.

Adjusting it

Everything on the device can also be set from a browser on the same Wi-Fi at http://flightscnr.local/ — range, units, accent colour, brightness, night dimming, a watch list for particular callsigns, and the alert rules. Changes apply straight away, without a reboot.

A quieter option lives in there too: it can be pointed at a local ADS-B receiver on the LAN instead of the internet feed, in which case an aerial on the roof is the only source and nothing leaves the building.

Is it watching us?

No. It has no camera and no microphone, and it only ever reads a public list of aircraft positions. The one thing it knows about this room is the coordinates someone typed in.

Why is that plane so low?

Altitude is above sea level, not above the car park. Around here the ground is already about 5,000 ft up, so a light aircraft in the pattern reads 6,000-something.

Please leave it plugged in

It is not a phone charger. Pulling the cable costs it a minute of finding the network and the sky again — and the room loses its favourite distraction.
